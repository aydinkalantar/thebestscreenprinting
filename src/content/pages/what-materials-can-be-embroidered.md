Embroidery is one of the most versatile ways to decorate a product. If a needle can pass through it and it can be held steady, there is a good chance it can be stitched. But not every material behaves the same under the needle.

## Materials that take embroidery well

- **Cotton.** The classic. Stable, easy to stitch and found in tees, polos and work shirts.
- **Polyester and blends.** Common in performance polos and uniforms. Holds stitches well and resists shrinking.
- **Fleece.** Hoodies and sweatshirts take embroidery beautifully, especially for small chest logos. See [hoodies and sweatshirts](/products/sweatshirts-hoodies/).
- **Twill.** The fabric behind most structured hats and many work jackets. Firm and ideal for crisp logos. See [hats](/products/hats/).
- **Denim.** Sturdy and great for jackets, shirts and jeans with a stitched detail.
- **Canvas.** Tote bags, backpacks and aprons. Thick and stable. See [bags](/products/bags/).
- **Towels.** Terry cloth can be embroidered for gyms, spas and gifts. See [towels](/products/towels/).
- **Wool.** Beanies and varsity-style jackets.
- **Leather.** Jackets, bags and accessories. It looks premium, but every needle hole is permanent, so the design must be right the first time.
- **Nylon.** Windbreakers and athletic gear. Lightweight and slippery, so it needs careful setup.
- **Felt and vinyl.** Often used for custom patches and appliques.
- **Silk.** Possible for special pieces, but delicate.

## Which materials are tricky to embroider?

Some fabrics need extra care:

- **Thin, stretchy knits** can pucker or distort without the right backing.
- **Very lightweight fabrics** may show the stabilizer or pull around dense designs.
- **High-pile fabrics** like terry and fleece can swallow small stitches, so a thin top layer is often used to keep details crisp.
- **Leather and vinyl** do not forgive mistakes, because holes cannot be undone.
- **Thick or seamed areas** can be hard to hoop.

The fix is usually the same: the right stabilizer, the right stitch density and a design sized for the material.

## How do embroidery machines work?

Commercial embroidery machines stitch designs automatically from a digital file. The key parts:

- **Hoop.** Holds the material tight while stitching. Hoop size limits how large a design can be in one pass.
- **Needles.** Move up and down to form stitches. Multi-needle machines hold several thread colors and switch between them automatically.
- **Thread.** Polyester, rayon, cotton, silk or metallic. Learn more in [what kind of thread is used for embroidery](/what-kind-of-thread-is-used-for-embroidery/).
- **Bobbin.** Holds the lower thread that locks each stitch from underneath.
- **Digitizing software.** Converts artwork into a stitch file that maps every stitch, its direction and its color.
- **Control screen.** Lets the operator check the design, size and position before stitching.

Machines range from home models to commercial multi-head machines that stitch several garments at once.

## Picking the right material for your logo

- **Small, detailed logo?** Choose a smooth, stable fabric like a cotton polo or twill hat.
- **Big, bold design on a soft garment?** Consider [screen printing](/screen-printing/) instead. Compare the two in [embroidery vs screen printing](/what-is-the-difference-between-embroidery-and-screen-printing/).
- **Uniforms?** Durable polyester and blends hold up to frequent washing. See [team uniforms and jerseys](/products/team-uniforms-jerseys/).
